A high-speed, bowling-inspired trip through the afterlife. I designed level flow and built track and navigation systems, including wrong-way feedback that follows the road beneath the player.

What I Owned

I led gameplay feature iteration across design sprints, built the guiding-light mechanic and checkpoint recovery, and developed a repeatable spline-based approach to track layout. I also refactored wrong-way detection when correct travel across differently authored splines triggered false warnings.

Featured work

Three selected parts of my work on Charon's Corner.

01 / Featured work

Designing spaces for a fast controller

Drifting and velocity chaining only feel good when the track gives players time to read the next turn. A path that looked clear in the editor could become confusing once the player reached it at speed.

I treated speed, sightlines, slopes, and obstacles as linked design constraints. Elevation changes and physical “pins” made a basic route more active while keeping the intended direction visible.

02 / Featured work

Making track construction repeatable

A single successful level was not enough for an 80-person team. The team needed rules that could produce more tracks without turning each section into a custom setup.

I built layouts from cascading spline paths so slope transitions and directional flow could be shaped consistently. The framework gave level designers a starting structure, while elevation changes and obstacles kept the route active. The wrong-way refactor also exposed an authoring rule: check each spline’s travel setting instead of assuming its knot order matches the course.

03 / Featured work

Making navigation feedback follow the road

At this speed, a missed turn or fall can cost the player their sense of direction. I added checkpoint recovery to return the player to a known position, plus a guiding light as a moving point of interest.

Direction testing exposed a different problem: the wrong-way warning appeared during correct travel in Level 3’s clothing section. Four splines were authored opposite to the course direction, and the detector searched nearby splines without confirming which road the player was on.

I corrected the four direction settings and changed detection to use the ground hit’s road and mesh segment. The player is a rolling marble, so the check compares horizontal travel velocity with that segment’s configured tangent. A Forward or Backward setting defines the route without reversing the track geometry.

I restored the UI component removed by a scene override and kept the existing warning delay. Controlled Unity play-mode checks covered correct and reverse travel on Level 3’s active roads, leaving the track, and the canvas appearing and hiding. Regression tests also covered shared road meshes, closed-loop seams, and scaled or rotated tracks; brief reversals stayed hidden.

Project takeaway

Charon’s Corner made me treat track construction and player feedback as one design problem. A spline can generate a usable road while still pointing opposite to the intended route. The wrong-way refactor taught me to check both the authored direction and the road the player is actually on before trusting a navigation warning.
